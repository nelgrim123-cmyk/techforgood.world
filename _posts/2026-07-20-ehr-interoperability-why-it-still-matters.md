Every hospital has an electronic health record system. Most patients still repeat their medical history at every new front desk. That gap — between having the technology and having it actually work for the person in front of it — is where digital health so often stalls.

Interoperability is not a technical detail. It is the difference between a clinician who sees the full picture and one working from a fragment. A patient's allergy list, their last set of labs, the medication a different provider prescribed last month — when that information does not travel with them, the system asks the patient to become their own record keeper, at the exact moment they are least equipped to do it.

## The appointment is where it starts

Long before a clinician opens a chart, the patient experience is already being shaped by something simpler: can they book, change, or cancel an appointment without calling a number that rings out. Scheduling friction is often dismissed as a minor operational detail, but it is usually the patient's first contact with an institution's digital maturity — and a fair predictor of what the rest of the visit will feel like.

Good scheduling systems do more than fill calendar slots. They reduce no-shows by matching reminder timing to how people actually behave, they surface the right visit type instead of a generic "book a consultation" button, and they quietly feed structured data back into the record instead of trapping it in a separate silo.

## What "good" looks like

The most effective digital point-of-care systems share a pattern: they are boring in the best sense. They do not ask patients or staff to think about the technology at all. The record is simply there, complete, at the moment of care. The appointment is simply booked, without a phone call. The technology's success is measured by how little anyone notices it.

That is a harder standard than it sounds. It means designing for the messiest real-world cases — a patient seen across three different providers, a system migration mid-year, a rural clinic with unreliable connectivity — rather than the clean demo scenario. It is unglamorous work. It is also, arguably, the work that matters most.
